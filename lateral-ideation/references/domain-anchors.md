# Domain Anchor Bank
 
A seed bank for Phase 2 (Hop with anchors). Use when no distant domain is jumping out on its own, or when you want a forced-random pull to escape the user's frame. The goal is a *foreign fixed point* to bridge from — the further from the problem's native domain, the better the odds of a non-obvious transfer.
 
## How to use this file
 
1. Take the structural abstraction from Phase 1 (e.g. "a routing-under-contention problem," "an allocation-under-uncertainty problem," "a trust-establishment-between-strangers problem").
2. Pick 3–5 anchors from the lists below — ideally from *different clusters*, and ideally none from the problem's home field.
3. For each, ask the bridging question: **"How does this domain handle the same structural tension, and what does that solution suggest here?"**
4. If you want true randomness, close your eyes and grab — an anchor you'd never have chosen is doing its job.
Prefer domains the user has not mentioned. If they're working on a trading system, hopping to "financial markets" is not a hop.
 
## Anchor clusters
 
### Living systems
Immunology (self/non-self discrimination, memory cells, tolerance), evolution (selection pressure, neutral drift, exaptation), ecology (predator-prey cycles, niche partitioning, keystone species), mycelial networks (decentralized resource routing), ant colonies (stigmergy, pheromone gradients), coral reefs (symbiosis, bleaching thresholds), the human microbiome (competitive exclusion), plant defense (induced resistance, allelopathy).
 
### Physical & material
Geology (stress, fault lines, sedimentation, phase transitions), fluid dynamics (turbulence, laminar flow, backpressure, cavitation), materials science (fatigue, annealing, composite layering, self-healing polymers), thermodynamics (gradients, equilibrium, dissipation), crystallography (defects that create useful properties), fracture mechanics (crack propagation, stress concentration).
 
### Movement of things & people
Logistics (just-in-time, buffer stock, bullwhip effect), air-traffic control (separation, sequencing, holding patterns), restaurant expediting (the pass, ticket rails, mise en place), supply chains (redundancy vs. efficiency), urban traffic (congestion pricing, induced demand), postal routing, epidemic spread (R₀, contact tracing, ring containment).
 
### Human systems & culture
Bureaucracy (form design, escalation paths, deliberate friction), law (precedent, burden of proof, statutes of limitation), diplomacy (face-saving, backchannels, hostage exchange), mythology (trickster archetypes, hero's journey, taboo), religious ritual (repetition, liminality, sacred/profane boundaries), etiquette (costly signaling), guild systems (apprenticeship, reputation).
 
### Art & craft
Music theory (tension/resolution, counterpoint, syncopation, key modulation), architecture (load paths, tensegrity, negative space, desire lines), film editing (the cut, montage, match-on-action), jazz improvisation (comping, trading fours, the head), poetry (constraint as generator — sonnet, haiku), cooking (emulsion, fermentation, Maillard, flavor bridging), textile weaving (warp/weft, tension).
 
### Games & competition
Game design (feedback loops, difficulty curves, dominant strategies, meaningful choice), poker (bluffing, pot odds, table image), chess (tempo, sacrifice, zugzwang), speedrunning (glitch exploitation, route optimization), sports tactics (pressing, zone vs. man, the counter-attack), auction theory (winner's curse, sniping).
 
### Information & control
Cryptography (zero-knowledge proofs, key exchange, one-way functions), error-correcting codes (redundancy, parity, graceful degradation), control theory (feedback, damping, PID, overshoot), signal processing (filtering, noise floor, sampling), distributed systems (consensus, eventual consistency, split-brain), compression (redundancy exploitation, lossy tradeoffs).
 
## Bridging prompts (when the anchor won't connect)
 
If an anchor feels inert, force it with one of these:
 
- **Inversion:** How would this domain *cause* the problem on purpose? Reverse that.
- **Constraint import:** What hard constraint does this domain live under, and what elegant move does that constraint force? Import the move.
- **Actor swap:** Who are the "agents" in this domain and what do they each want? Map them onto the problem's actors.
- **Failure mode:** How does this domain fail catastrophically, and what does it do to prevent that? The prevention is often the idea.
- **Scale shift:** How does this domain solve the tension at a very different scale (cellular vs. planetary)? Borrow the small or large version.
## A worked hop (for calibration)
 
**Problem:** users abandon a multi-step signup flow.
**Abstracted:** a *momentum-preservation problem* — a system that loses energy at every transition and needs to keep an entity moving through friction.
 
- **Anchor — luge/bobsled:** speed is banked *before* the hard part; the run is designed so gravity does the work through the technical section. → Front-load the easiest, most rewarding step so the user carries momentum into the tedious fields.
- **Anchor — Maillard reaction (cooking):** the good stuff only happens above a threshold; below it, nothing. → Identify the activation threshold — the single step where committed users diverge from bouncers — and pour all the reduction of friction there, not evenly.
- **Anchor — air-traffic holding patterns:** you don't drop a plane that isn't ready to land; you park it in a loop and bring it back. → Don't force completion in one pass; let users park a half-finished signup and get pulled back with the context preserved.
Three anchors, three structurally-grounded directions, none of which is "add a progress bar." That's the difference between anchored hopping and chaos.
